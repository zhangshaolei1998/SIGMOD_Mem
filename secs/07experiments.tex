\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

We evaluate the three claims made in the Introduction: the evolving
memory hierarchy, query-specific pipelines with experience-guided
grounding, and cost-aware execution. Exp-1--2 assess answer quality and
transfer; Exp-3 examines the pipelines' evidence requirements; Exp-4--8 examine
cost-aware execution, the hierarchy, second-order Agent--Memory
experience, plan classification, and history-length effects. Exp-9
connects these mechanisms across successive queries.

\subsection{Experimental Setup}
\label{subsec:setup}

\input{tables/datasets}

\stitle{Datasets.}
Table~\ref{tbl:benchmarks} groups eight benchmarks by memory ability.
\locomo{} tests multi-session dialogue~\cite{locomo}; \lmev{} tests
environment experience from web-agent trajectories~\cite{longmemevalv2}.
\lmeo{}, \lmes{}, and \lmem{} vary evidence availability and history
length~\cite{longmemeval}. \evomem{} tests adaptation across task
streams~\cite{evomemory}; \dynamicmem{} tracks evolving user
profiles~\cite{dynamicmem}.
We use \membench{}~\cite{membench} to study single-fact retrieval,
independent composition, dependent composition, and cost-aware
execution. Together, these benchmarks evaluate the memory hierarchy and its
accumulation of user-specific evidence and second-order Agent--Memory
experience over time.

\stitle{Baselines.}
The seven baselines are Full-Context,
RAG~\cite{rag}, MemGPT~\cite{memgpt}, Mem0 and
Mem0$^{g}$~\cite{mem0}, Zep~\cite{zep}, and
LongMemEval-Fw~\cite{longmemeval}. They provide, respectively,
full-history prompting, passage retrieval, context tiers,
consolidated facts and entity relations, temporal graphs, and an
indexing--retrieval--reading pipeline.
We compare methods under a shared backbone and complement the overall
comparison with component ablations.

\stitle{Metrics and Settings.}
Multiple-choice tasks use exact match; open-answer tasks use F1 with
benchmark-specific normalization. Comparisons are within a benchmark's
metric scale.
The default backbone is GPT-5.5; Exp-2 covers four closed-source and
four open-source models. Every reported call count uses $C(q)$ from
Eq.~\eqref{eq:cost}; averages report mean $C(q)$, and totals report
$\sum_q C(q)$.

\subsection{Exp-1. Answer Quality Across Memory Abilities}
\label{subsec:exp1}

Table~\ref{tbl:main_results} shows that \sys{} matches or improves the
strongest evaluated baseline in every column. The mean benchmark-score
margin is $2.6$ points.
The gains vary with the memory ability being tested.

\stitle{Evidence organization matters most over long histories.}
The margins on \lmeo{}, \lmes{}, and \lmem{} are $5.0$, $5.6$, and
$4.9$ points. A query over many sessions has to recover the right
entities and temporal versions before computing its answer.
The architecture in Section~\ref{sec:overview} separates this source
history from reusable fact values; the pipelines in
Section~\ref{sec:framework} then expose coverage and binding obligations.

This reduces the amount of evidence alignment left implicit in a long
generation, a difficulty also observed in long-context
evaluations~\cite{longcontext,ruler}.
The margin persists at the largest evaluated history length, consistent
with this separation helping beyond short histories.
Exp-5 supplies the complementary evidence that removing first-order
facts or composition pipelines lowers accuracy.
Together, the length trend and the ablations localize the advantage to
two stages of the method: the hierarchy turns source history into reusable
fact versions, and the pipelines turn those facts into explicit coverage
and binding checks. Long histories amplify both benefits because the system
avoids rediscovering the same operands and recomputing their relations inside
one unconstrained generation.

\stitle{Overall scores leave the role of computation unresolved.}
The margins are $0.6$ points on \locomo{} and $1.6$ on \membench{}.
An individual lookup requires identity and source support; a population
request additionally requires coverage and a supported judgment for each
candidate. A benchmark-level score averages these different obligations,
so it cannot identify which computation produced a gain. Exp-3 traces the
evidence boundary directly, and Exp-7 tests whether selecting among the
three computations matters end to end.

\stitle{Overall state accuracy can hide second-order evolution.}
On \evomem{}, \sys{} gains $2.5$ points over the strongest baseline;
on \dynamicmem{}, both systems score $34.9$.
These aggregates combine early queries with later queries that can reuse
accumulated experience. Exp-6 separates the positions in a user's query
history, and Exp-5 isolates second-order Agent--Memory experience at
unchanged $\sum_q C(q)$.

\finding{Long-horizon workloads show the largest gains; ablations link
them more directly to representation and pipelines than overall rankings do.}

\subsection{Exp-2. Transfer Across Language Models}
\label{subsec:exp2}

We hold \membench{} and the reported configuration fixed and compare
against the strongest baseline rerun with each backbone.

\input{tables/main_results_open}

The gains in Table~\ref{tbl:main_results_open} range from $1.6$ to
$5.9$ points for closed-source models and from $5.8$ to $7.9$ for
open-source models. Their group means are $3.9$ and $7.0$ points.
Within each group, the gain increases as the prior-baseline score falls.
The advantage therefore extends beyond the default model's particular
way of using memory.

Across backbones, memory identifies values and exposes the required
composition before answer generation. The fixed-configuration comparison
shows that the advantage transfers across the evaluated models. Because
each backbone is compared under the same memory configuration, the widening
margin as baseline quality falls is consistent with the pipelines moving
coverage, alignment, and combination out of the final free-form generation.
The memory system supplies a structured intermediate result, so each
backbone need not reconstruct the computation from retrieved text.

\finding{Gains transfer across the evaluated backbones and increase as
baseline scores fall within each group, consistent with a reduced
evidence-processing burden.}

\subsection{Exp-3. Evidence Requirements of Query-Specific Pipelines}
\label{subsec:exp3}

The three pipelines differ in the evidence obligations they expose.
Single-fact retrieval terminates once the requested value and its support
are established. Independent composition additionally requires population
coverage and a supported judgment for each candidate. Dependent composition
preserves the event--value bindings needed by a later selection or lookup.

\stitle{Independent composition requires population coverage.}
A count can be wrong even when the retrieved passages are topically
relevant: candidates can be omitted or duplicated, and a mentioned
event may not satisfy the requested predicate.
The independent-composition pipeline in Eq.~\eqref{eq:new-reduce} separates
enumeration, per-candidate judgment, and tallying.
In Alice's example, establishing that pottery was indoors justifies its
exclusion from the outdoor count. Once each judgment is supported,
arithmetic need not be another unstructured model inference.

\stitle{Dependent composition preserves value--event bindings.}
A dependent composition must associate each normalized value with its event
before ordering them. Retrieving both durations without that alignment
can still select the wrong activity. When the selected activity determines
an organizer request, Eq.~\eqref{eq:new-compose} carries the required
binding into the next lookup.

\input{tables/case_study}

Table~\ref{tbl:casestudy} shows the independent-composition boundary.
Full-Context leaves counting implicit and returns 5; Zep leaves the
predicate unresolved and returns 4; \sys{} evaluates the candidates
explicitly and returns 3. The trace exposes the coverage, candidate
evaluation, and combination required by the pipeline. The two incorrect
answers arise at different stages: one skips the count structure, while
the other does not resolve the predicate. Independent composition makes
both stages observable before combination, so additional retrieval cannot
silently substitute for a missing judgment.

\finding{The pipeline boundary follows the evidence obligation: one
supported value, a covered candidate set, or an answer-dependent binding.}

\subsection{Exp-4. Cost-Aware Execution}
\label{subsec:exp4}

Table~\ref{tbl:cost} separates two sources of savings in cost-aware
execution: allocating effort across queries and selectively refining
evidence only while it remains worthwhile.

\stitle{Query-specific budget allocation avoids uniform repetition.}
Fixed Budget ($K{=}3$) has $\sum_q C(q)=900$ at $89.1$ accuracy.
The allocation-only configuration has $\sum_q C(q)=532$ at $92.8$:
$368$ lower $\sum_q C(q)$ ($40.9\%$) with a higher score.
The result shows that a fixed per-query budget spends calls independently
of the evidence state. Query-specific allocation instead reserves work for
unresolved obligations; fewer calls and higher accuracy occur together
because effort moves to queries whose evidence can still change the result.

\stitle{Selective refinement removes work after allocation.}
Selective refinement reduces $\sum_q C(q)$ from $532$ to $444$, saving another $88$
($16.5\%$ of the allocation-only count), with the same reported aggregate
accuracy. Average $C(q)$ is $1.00$ for single-fact retrieval, $2.30$ for
independent composition, and $2.18$ for dependent composition.

The realized costs fall below their allocated maxima. In total,
$\sum_q C(q)$ falls by $456$ relative to Fixed Budget ($K{=}3$),
giving the reported $50.7\%$ reduction.
These controls separate allocation from realized expenditure. The
action-level rule in Section~\ref{sec:executor} compares predicted
evidence resolution with cost. Alice's verified duration comparison
needs no new semantic judgment, whereas her ambiguous outing needs
additional source evidence.
Allocation therefore acts before execution by choosing a work budget;
selective refinement acts during execution by releasing work after an
obligation is discharged. Their separate savings show that cost-aware
execution improves both where calls are assigned and how many reserved
calls are ultimately used.

Figure~\ref{fig:pareto} summarizes the accuracy--cost tradeoff under $C(q)$.
The cost model in Eq.~\eqref{eq:cost} records the memory-side work used
to establish an answer and distinguishes cold acquisition from warm reuse.

\finding{Budget allocation reduces $\sum_q C(q)$ by $368$ against
Fixed Budget ($K{=}3$); selective refinement removes another $88$ at unchanged
aggregate accuracy.}

\subsection{Exp-5. Architecture and Execution Ablations}
\label{subsec:exp5}

Table~\ref{tbl:ablation} isolates the contributions of the memory
hierarchy, query-specific pipelines, second-order Agent--Memory experience,
and cost-aware execution.

\stitle{Organized facts and pipelines both matter.}
Removing first-order facts lowers accuracy by $6.1$ points on
\membench{} and $2.7$ on \lmev{}.
Removing composition pipelines lowers it by $4.9$ and $2.0$; removing query-plan classification
lowers it by $3.7$ and $1.5$.
Each configuration also has a larger $\sum_q C(q)$ than the full system.

The loss therefore cannot be explained by a smaller $C(q)$. First-order
facts provide the input to explicit composition;
query-plan classification determines which computation to apply.
Raw-session grounding retains source details that have not yet been
acquired as first-order facts. The separately observed losses support
both representation and processing. Removing first-order facts forces
repeated grounding from raw sessions, increasing $\sum_q C(q)$ while
degrading the operands supplied to composition. Removing composition or
plan classification also increases cost but still lowers accuracy, showing
that more calls do not replace the missing computation structure.
\stitle{Second-order experience changes grounding at the same $C(q)$.}
Removing second-order experience loses $0.7$ points on \membench{} and
$1.7$ on \lmev{}, while $\sum_q C(q)$ remains $444$ and $602$.
Removing experience-guided priority loses $0.3$ and $1.0$, again without changing
those totals. These contrasts link access experience to better scores
at the same aggregate cost.

Equation~\eqref{eq:pi} expresses the access principle at fact-version
granularity: relevance identifies eligible evidence and remembered
use changes its order. The ablations measure that principle through
the system's second-order experience and priority controls. Because the
call totals are fixed, these gains come from choosing and ordering evidence
more effectively, not from allocating additional inference.

\stitle{Allocation and selective refinement have different effects.}
Without cost-aware allocation, $\sum_q C(q)$ increases by $312$
on \membench{} and $301$ on \lmev{}; accuracy falls by $0.3$ on the
former and is unchanged on the latter.
Without selective refinement, $\sum_q C(q)$ increases by $88$ and $121$
without changing the reported scores. Allocation affects both quality and
cost; selective refinement removes work after allocation.

\finding{The memory hierarchy and query-specific pipelines contribute to
answer quality; second-order experience improves grounding at unchanged
$\sum_q C(q)$.}

\subsection{Exp-6. Performance Across a User's Query History}
\label{subsec:exp6}

Table~\ref{tbl:second_order_learning} groups answer quality by position
in each user's query history. At later positions, the architecture has
accumulated both user evidence and second-order Agent--Memory experience.

On \evomem{}, \sys{} rises from $22.2$ to $27.8$; on \dynamicmem{},
from $29.1$ to $32.8$. All four position buckets increase on both
benchmarks, whereas the strongest baseline remains nearly flat.

This trend is consistent with the intended role of second-order
Agent--Memory experience.
Closed conversations extend raw history; queries add facts and second-order
experience. Alice's durations enrich content, while their reported use
and acquisition cost guide later access. Content growth and second-order
experience are therefore distinct explanations of improvement.

The fixed-$C(q)$ experience ablations in Exp-5 complement this trajectory:
they isolate the contribution of second-order experience at unchanged
$\sum_q C(q)$, while the position trend measures the full evolving memory
state. The two views separate accumulation from utilization: later queries
have more stored evidence, but removing $\Uu$ still loses accuracy when the
aggregate call budget is held fixed. The improvement over query position
therefore aligns with the paper's central distinction between remembering
more about the user and learning how to use that memory.

\finding{Performance improves across a user's query history. Fixed-$C(q)$
ablations separately show that second-order experience contributes at
unchanged $\sum_q C(q)$.}

\subsection{Exp-7. Query-Plan Classification}
\label{subsec:exp7routing}

\input{tables/routing}

The rule-based plan compiler in Table~\ref{tbl:routing} reaches $91.4\%$
plan-classification accuracy with $C_{\mathrm{plan}}(q)=0$.
The LLM plan compiler reaches $95.1\%$, a $3.7$-point increase, but improves
end-to-end accuracy by only $0.4$ points.
Its $C_{\mathrm{plan}}(q)=1$ adds $300$ to $\sum_q C(q)$, or
$67.6\%$ of the full system's $\sum_q C(q)=444$.

Plan-classification accuracy and answer accuracy capture complementary
aspects of execution. A corrected route improves the computation made
available to the answerer, while evidence grounding determines the
final answer.
Always choosing \point{} loses $5.3$ end-to-end points.
The three-way partition supports making the required computation explicit,
while showing a small return from additional compilation complexity. These
two comparisons separate the value of the plan space from the sophistication
of its compiler: selecting among all three pipeline classes is important,
whereas paying for an LLM on every classification produces little additional
end-to-end benefit.

\finding{The more accurate plan compiler adds $300$ to $\sum_q C(q)$ for a $0.4$-point
end-to-end gain; selecting a suitable computation matters more here.}

\subsection{Exp-8. Scaling with History Length}
\label{subsec:exp8}

\input{tables/scaling}

Table~\ref{tbl:scaling} increases history from $40$K to $1.5$M tokens,
a $37.5\times$ range. Average $C(q)$ remains $1.9$ for \sys{}
and $1.0$ for Full-Context. Yet latency grows $20.7\times$ for
Full-Context, from $2.3$s to $47.6$s, and only $1.09\times$ for
\sys{}, from $3.2$s to $3.5$s. $C(q)$ alone therefore understates
the differences in inference expense.

At $40$K, structured memory access is slower by $0.9$s. At $115$K,
\sys{} takes $3.4$s against $5.8$s, and at $1.5$M it is
$13.6\times$ faster. Structured access has a fixed cost at short histories;
reusable facts and bounded evidence processing avoid placing the entire
source history in each query as that history grows.

Latency captures work that $C(q)$ does not. Full-Context uses one call at
every length, yet that call processes an increasingly long prompt. \sys{}
uses an average of $1.9$ calls, but their inputs are selected
facts and unresolved evidence blocks whose size is decoupled from the complete
history. The hierarchy therefore controls context growth, while the executor
controls repeated semantic work.

Accuracy advantages over Full-Context remain $17.9$, $21.3$, and
$16.7$ points across the three variants.
The pipelines retain explicit coverage and binding checks while reusing
available facts; new evidence is acquired only when it is required.

\finding{The query-time advantage grows with history length as structured
memory avoids repeatedly processing the full conversation history.}

\subsection{Exp-9. End-to-End Case Study}
\label{subsec:exp9}

We trace Alice's state across the three queries in Examples~1--3. Each
turn shows the selected pipeline, the retrieved memory, and the updates to
raw sessions $\Hh$, first-order facts $\Ff$, and second-order experience
$\Uu$.

\input{tables/evolution_case}

Table~\ref{tbl:evolution-case} makes the two kinds of evolution visible.
Content evolves from settings and organizer links to durations and the
rain-altered outing's actual setting. Operations evolve from the evidence
returned and consumed in each turn: Turn~1 distinguishes exposure from use;
Turn~2 records a successful acquisition and binding; Turn~3 reuses the
completed judgments and directs refinement to the only new gap. The later
queries therefore inherit reusable operands and experience about how those
operands were obtained and used.

\stitle{What changes from one turn to the next.}
After Turn~1, $\Ff$ supplies settings and organizers without reconstructing
$\Hh$; $\Uu$ records six returned facts but five used facts. Turn~2 acquires
the missing durations and retains the successful comparison and binding.
Turn~3 reuses those judgments and evaluates only the new outing. Thus $\Hh$
grows per closed turn, $\Ff$ per reusable value, and $\Uu$ per execution.

The trace also separates reuse from support. Reusing the three earlier
judgments reduces reacquisition, and the warm comparison needs $K(q)=0$
additional memory-side calls. Neither fact authorizes an exact answer for a
changed population. Turn~3 becomes supported only after its fourth setting
judgment is established; otherwise the pipeline returns $[2,3]$ as partial.
Together, first-order memory avoids repeated extraction, second-order
experience focuses effort, and the selected pipeline preserves the current
query's evidence obligations.

\finding{Across three turns, $\Hh$ preserves completed conversations,
$\Ff$ makes verified facts reusable, and $\Uu$ changes later grounding and
effort while each pipeline retains its evidence obligations.}
