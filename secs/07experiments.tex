\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

We examine evidence organization and pipelines (Exp-1--3 and Exp-5),
persistent access experience (Exp-5--6), and selective execution
(Exp-4, Exp-7, and Exp-8). Component controls complement overall scores;
Section~\ref{subsec:setup} defines which mechanisms the prototype measures.

\subsection{Experimental Setup}
\label{subsec:setup}

\stitle{Evaluation Scope.}
The evaluated prototype uses \point{}, \agg{}, and \cmp{} as benchmark
patterns, not the normalized dependency classes in
Section~\ref{sec:framework}. It acquires session-level facts on first
access, uses coarser workload experience than the fact-version credit
specified here, and applies pattern-dependent reservations with
agreement-based stopping.
These measurements test evidence organization, workload-guided access,
and selective execution, not the formal model's finer acquisition,
normalized-plan routing, fact-version credit, or action-value estimator.
Those policies require targeted evaluation.
In the tables, a benchmark query is one question $Q$; it is not an
internal memory query $q$. Uniform-$K{=}3$ names three read-path
executions per benchmark question, not the adaptive reservation $K(q)$.

\input{tables/datasets}

\stitle{Datasets.}
Table~\ref{tbl:benchmarks} groups eight benchmarks by the ability they
stress. \locomo{}~\cite{locomo} and \lmev{}~\cite{longmemeval} test
multi-session recall. \lmeo{}, \lmes{}, and \lmem{}~\cite{longmemeval}
evaluate related tasks at increasing history lengths.
\evomem{}~\cite{evomemory} and \dynamicmem{}~\cite{dynamicmem} contain
repeated questions over evolving user state.
\membench{}~\cite{membench} contains $300$ benchmark questions in the reported
evaluation and supplies the pattern groups for the routing and cost
controls. These settings expose different pressures: source access
over long histories, computation over multiple facts, and accumulated
experience across a user's requests.

\stitle{Baselines.}
The seven baselines are Full-Context~\cite{longcontext},
RAG~\cite{rag}, MemGPT~\cite{memgpt}, Mem0 and
Mem0$^{g}$~\cite{mem0}, Zep~\cite{zep}, and
LongMemEval-Fw~\cite{longmemeval}. They provide, respectively,
full-history prompting, passage retrieval, context tiers,
consolidated facts and entity relations, temporal graphs, and an
indexing--retrieval--reading pipeline.
The reported comparison uses released implementations under a shared
backbone. Prompts and implementation choices also differ, so component
ablations complement the overall system comparison.

\stitle{Metrics and Settings.}
Multiple-choice tasks use exact match; open-answer tasks use F1, with
phrase-level normalization for \lmev{} and token-level normalization
elsewhere. Comparisons are within a benchmark's metric scale.
The default backbone is GPT-5.5; Exp-2 covers four closed-source and
four open-source models~\cite{gpt4,claude,gemini}.
The prototype uses Python and an OpenAI-compatible client.
Reported control settings are reused across benchmarks and backbones.
Read-path calls are counted per benchmark question and exclude fact
acquisition; Exp-4 distinguishes this metric from the full memory-query ledger in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:cost}.
The reported totals are not an acquisition-inclusive ledger or a
measurement of separately accounted Agent-side calls.

\subsection{Exp-1. Answer Scores Across Memory Abilities}
\label{subsec:exp1}

Table~\ref{tbl:main_results} shows that \sys{} matches or improves the
strongest evaluated baseline in every column. The mean benchmark-score
margin is $2.6$ points; this is an arithmetic mean of within-benchmark
differences, not a pooled accuracy across different metrics.
The distribution of gains is more informative than the mean alone.

\stitle{Evidence organization matters most over long histories.}
The margins on \lmeo{}, \lmes{}, and \lmem{} are $5.0$, $5.6$, and
$4.9$ points. A query over many sessions has to recover the right
entities and temporal versions before computing its answer.
The architecture in Section~\ref{sec:overview} separates this source
history from reusable fact values; the pipelines in
Section~\ref{sec:framework} then expose coverage and binding obligations.
This reduces the amount of evidence alignment left implicit in a long
generation, a difficulty also observed in long-context
evaluations~\cite{longcontext,ruler}.
The maintained margin at the largest scale is consistent with this
separation, rather than a benefit restricted to short histories.
Exp-5 supplies the complementary evidence that removing the fact
representation or explicit operators lowers accuracy.

\stitle{Overall scores do not identify which computations improve.}
The margins are $0.6$ points on \locomo{} and $1.6$ on \membench{}.
These averages do not establish the workloads' plan-class distributions.
An individual lookup requires identity/version selection and source
support, whereas a population request also needs candidate computation.
On \lmev{}, $10.3$ versus $9.4$ gives a $9.6\%$ relative improvement,
but its phrase-level F1 scale prevents interpreting the absolute gap
as equivalent to the multiple-choice gaps.
Exp-3 tests the within-pattern distinction more directly.

\stitle{Aggregate state accuracy can hide operational evolution.}
On \evomem{}, \sys{} gains $2.5$ points over the strongest baseline;
on \dynamicmem{}, it ties that baseline at $34.9$.
This tie is important: repeated interaction does not imply a universal
aggregate gain. An overall score mixes early queries with sparse
experience and later queries that can reuse it.
The position analysis in Exp-6 examines that trajectory, while Exp-5
tests whether workload experience matters at unchanged call totals.

\finding{The overall advantage is strongest on long-horizon workloads.
Representation and pipeline ablations provide a more direct link to
the technical mechanisms than the aggregate ranking alone.}

\subsection{Exp-2. Transfer Across Backbones}
\label{subsec:exp2}

We hold \membench{} and the reported configuration fixed and compare
against the strongest baseline rerun with each backbone.

\input{tables/main_results_open}

The gains in Table~\ref{tbl:main_results_open} range from $1.6$ to
$5.9$ points for closed-source models and from $5.8$ to $7.9$ for
open-source models. Their group means are $3.9$ and $7.0$ points.
Within each group, the gain increases as the prior-baseline score falls.
Thus the advantage is not restricted to the default model's particular
way of using memory.

Explicit evidence processing provides a possible explanation: memory
identifies values and exposes computation instead of leaving entity
alignment, counting, and answering to one generation. This can help a
weaker backbone, but source ambiguity and semantic judgments remain.
The gains support transfer across models; they do not identify which
internal capability causes the difference.

\finding{Gains transfer across all evaluated backbones and are larger
for lower-scoring models within each group, consistent with explicit
evidence processing reducing the burden on the backbone.}

\subsection{Exp-3. Accuracy by Evidence Computation}
\label{subsec:exp3}

\input{tables/perclass}

Within Table~\ref{tbl:perclass}, \sys{} exceeds the strongest prior
score by $0.3$ points on point lookup, $6.1$ on aggregation, and
$3.1$ on comparison. The difference between these gaps links the
results to the pipelines more clearly than a single overall score.

\stitle{Aggregation needs coverage, not just relevance.}
A count can be wrong even when the retrieved passages are topically
relevant: candidates can be omitted or duplicated, and a mentioned
event may not satisfy the requested predicate.
The population pipeline in Eq.~\eqref{eq:new-reduce} separates
enumeration, per-candidate judgment, and tallying.
In Alice's example, pottery must be examined and excluded, not simply
dropped because the desired answer concerns outdoor activities.
Once each judgment is supported, arithmetic need not be another
unstructured model inference.
The larger aggregation gap is consistent with making these operations
explicit, although the table does not individually measure coverage
errors and predicate errors.

\stitle{Comparison needs aligned values; composition needs bindings.}
A comparison must associate each normalized value with its event
before ordering them. Retrieving both durations without that alignment
can still select the wrong activity.
For independent candidates this is an independent reduction; when its
winner determines an organizer request, Eq.~\eqref{eq:new-compose}
adds a dependent binding.
The comparison scores support explicit value processing, but do not
separately test that binding step. Establishing the new plan boundary
requires dependency annotations, not relabeling the original comparison
group as \comp{}.

\input{tables/case_study}

A separate benchmark trace in Table~\ref{tbl:casestudy} shows the distinction:
Full-Context leaves counting implicit and returns 5; Zep leaves the
predicate unresolved and returns 4; the prototype makes candidate
decisions explicit and returns 3 after two agreeing executions.
The trace illustrates why a relevant representation alone is not a
complete computation. It also separates the roles of access experience,
candidate evaluation, and stopping, without proving that each one
independently caused the correct answer.

The reported per-pattern scores have an unresolved grouping/averaging
provenance: they cannot be read as a micro-accuracy decomposition of
Table~\ref{tbl:main_results} under shared class weights.
The comparisons above are within Table~\ref{tbl:perclass};
attributing a fraction of the overall gain to a pattern requires the
original per-query records.

\finding{The largest within-pattern gap is on aggregation, where
coverage and candidate decisions must precede tallying. Comparison
supports explicit value alignment, not a separate test of dependent
bindings.}

\subsection{Exp-4. Where Execution Savings Come From}
\label{subsec:exp4}

Table~\ref{tbl:cost} separates two sources of read-path savings:
selecting which queries receive repeated execution, and stopping before
all allocated repetitions are spent.

\stitle{Selective allocation avoids paying for uniform repetition.}
Uniform-$K{=}3$ uses $900$ calls at $89.1$ accuracy.
The budget-only prototype uses $532$ calls at $92.8$:
$368$ fewer calls ($40.9\%$) with a higher score.
This comparison changes evidence processing as well as allocation,
so it does not attribute the accuracy gain to budget selection alone.
It does show that a larger global call count is not sufficient to
recover the prototype's accuracy. Repetition must be attached to
useful evidence work.

\stitle{Stopping removes work after the allocation decision.}
Agreement stopping reduces $532$ calls to $444$, saving another $88$
($16.5\%$ of the budget-only count), with the same reported aggregate
accuracy. Point lookups remain at one call; aggregation and comparison
average $2.30$ and $2.18$ instead of three.
These unequal realized costs show that an allocated maximum need not
be fully spent. In total, $456$ calls are saved relative to Uniform-$K{=}3$,
giving the reported $50.7\%$ reduction.
The equality of aggregate scores does not imply identical predictions.

These controls motivate the action-level decision in
Section~\ref{sec:executor}: allocate to unresolved work, not just a query
label, and compare another action's predicted evidence-gap reduction
with its cost. Alice's verified duration comparison needs no new
semantic judgment; her ambiguous outing requires new source
evidence rather than more votes over the same fragment.
Agreement-based savings support selective stopping as a design
direction, but do not establish the accuracy of the new action forecasts.

Figure~\ref{fig:pareto} summarizes the reported read-path tradeoff.
The accounting excludes fact acquisition, including properties extracted
from an old session in response to a new query. This is not necessarily
a one-time session cost. Full $C(q)$ in Eq.~\eqref{eq:cost} must charge
such work where it occurs and distinguish cold acquisition from warm
reuse; the reported savings cannot yet be extended to that full ledger.

\finding{The budget-only prototype uses $368$ fewer calls than
Uniform-$K{=}3$; stopping saves another $88$ at unchanged aggregate
accuracy. These configurations motivate selective execution, while
full acquisition costs remain outside the reported metric.}

\subsection{Exp-5. Which Mechanisms Matter}
\label{subsec:exp5}

Table~\ref{tbl:ablation} groups prototype modules, not the three memory
levels $\Hh,\Ff,\Uu$. The differences expose distinct roles for evidence
organization, experience-guided access, and execution controls.

\stitle{Organized facts and pipelines both matter.}
Removing the fact representation lowers accuracy by $6.1$ points on
\membench{} and $2.7$ on \lmev{}.
Removing operators lowers it by $4.9$ and $2.0$; removing the planner
lowers it by $3.7$ and $1.5$.
Each configuration also issues more calls than the full prototype.
The loss therefore cannot be explained simply by having fewer inference
calls. Reusable identities and values provide the input to explicit
computation; routing selects which computation to apply.
The separately observed losses support both representation and processing.
They are not additive contributions: changing one module changes the
evidence and work seen by other modules.
Nor does this table directly test recovery of a newly requested property
from raw sessions.

\stitle{Experience changes evidence use at the same call count.}
Removing workload experience loses $0.7$ points on \membench{} and
$1.7$ on \lmev{}, while total calls remain $444$ and $602$.
Removing priority ranking loses $0.3$ and $1.0$, again without changing
those totals. These contrasts are especially relevant to our core
claim: better memory operation need not mean more model calls.
The larger score change on the open-answer benchmark is consistent
with evidence choice affecting which facts reach the answer.
It is not an error-type analysis, and the metric scales differ.

In the formal model, Eq.~\eqref{eq:pi} makes the analogous
access mechanism explicit: current relevance identifies eligible facts,
while remembered use changes their order. The measured experience
controls support workload-guided access, but do not separately verify
the fact-version credit rule or rule out changes in cache contents.

\stitle{Allocation and stopping have different effects.}
Without the measured pattern-aware budget, calls increase by $312$
on \membench{} and $301$ on \lmev{}; accuracy falls by $0.3$ on the
former and is unchanged on the latter.
Without early stopping, calls increase by $88$ and $121$, with unchanged
aggregate accuracy. Thus allocation can affect both quality and expense,
whereas stopping is accuracy-neutral in these reported aggregates.
The session-eviction control adds $49$ calls on \lmev{} for a
$0.2$-point change. Its original scope is not documented here;
it should not be interpreted as removing the durable raw evidence
needed by the architecture in Section~\ref{sec:overview}.

\finding{Representation and pipelines lose accuracy despite more calls
when removed; workload experience loses accuracy at unchanged call
totals. The two mechanisms cannot be reduced to a larger inference budget.}

\subsection{Exp-6. Does Access Improve Across a User's Queries?}
\label{subsec:exp6}

Table~\ref{tbl:second_order_learning} restricts to users with at least
ten queries and groups each query by its position in that user's
benchmark-question sequence. This subset is different from the full evaluation population;
its absolute scores should not be substituted for the overall scores.

On \evomem{}, \sys{} rises from $22.2$ to $27.8$; on \dynamicmem{},
from $29.1$ to $32.8$. The gains are $5.6$ and $3.7$ points.
The strongest baseline, Zep, changes by only $0.2$ and $-0.1$ points
between the same endpoint buckets. Consequently, \sys{}'s gap to Zep
widens from $0.4$ to $5.8$ on \evomem{} and moves from $-0.8$ to
$3.0$ on \dynamicmem{}.
All four \sys{} buckets increase monotonically, whereas the evaluated
baselines show only small fluctuations.

This is the cross-question behavior second-order memory seeks to produce.
Closed conversations extend raw history; queries add facts and operational
experience. Alice's durations enrich content, while their reported use
and acquisition cost guide later access. Content growth and operational
experience are therefore distinct explanations of improvement.

The fixed-call experience ablations in Exp-5 and the position trend
address complementary questions: experience affects scores without
more aggregate calls, and scores improve as more experience becomes
available. Together they support the role of persistent access feedback
more strongly than either table alone.
They still do not isolate fact-level use from cache growth and query
difficulty. The backbone and prompt are fixed across buckets, but those
other factors are not. Shuffled-order, fixed-cache, and difficulty-matched
controls are needed for a causal attribution to operational evolution.

\finding{The advantage widens across the observed position buckets.
Together with fixed-call experience ablations, this supports persistent
access adaptation, while leaving cache growth and query-order effects
to be separated.}

\subsection{Exp-7. The Value and Cost of Routing}
\label{subsec:exp7routing}

\input{tables/routing}

The rule-based router in Table~\ref{tbl:routing} reaches $91.4\%$
pattern accuracy at zero LLM calls.
The LLM router reaches $95.1\%$, a $3.7$-point increase, but improves
end-to-end accuracy by only $0.4$ points.
Its extra call for each of $300$ queries adds $300$ calls, or
$67.6\%$ of the full prototype's $444$-call count.

Routing accuracy and answer accuracy therefore do not improve
one-for-one. A corrected route can still encounter missing source
evidence or a wrong predicate judgment; some route differences may
also leave the final answer unchanged.
These are possible mechanisms, not observed error counts.
By contrast, always choosing \point{} loses $5.3$ end-to-end points.
The comparison supports making the required computation explicit,
while showing a small measured return from further router complexity
in this workload.

The lower block perturbs the original agreement threshold $\theta$
and priority weight $\alpha$; accuracy ranges span at most $0.6$ points.
They control retained repetitions and evidence priority. The modest
variation suggests tolerance, not statistical equivalence or freedom
from calibration. Their definitions differ from normalized-plan routing
and action-value allocation, so these scores do not validate those policies.

\finding{Selecting a nontrivial computation matters more here than
paying for the more accurate router: the latter adds $300$ calls for
a $0.4$-point end-to-end gain.}

\subsection{Exp-8. How History Length Changes the Tradeoff}
\label{subsec:exp8}

\input{tables/scaling}

Table~\ref{tbl:scaling} increases history from $40$K to $1.5$M tokens,
a $37.5\times$ range. Reported calls per benchmark question remain $1.9$ for \sys{}
and $1.0$ for Full-Context. Yet latency grows $20.7\times$ for
Full-Context, from $2.3$s to $47.6$s, and only $1.09\times$ for
\sys{}, from $3.2$s to $3.5$s. Call count alone is therefore not a
complete description of inference expense.

At $40$K, structured memory access is slower by $0.9$s.
At $115$K, \sys{} takes $3.4$s against $5.8$s, and at $1.5$M
it is $13.6\times$ faster.
This crossover makes the architectural tradeoff concrete:
organizing and accessing evidence adds overhead, but need not repeatedly
place the entire source history inside the answering prompt.
Reusable facts and bounded evidence-processing steps provide a
plausible explanation for the measured latency trend.
The table does not report per-call token lengths or separate acquisition
time, so it cannot directly attribute the trend to either one.

Accuracy advantages over Full-Context remain $17.9$, $21.3$, and
$16.7$ points across the three variants.
The formal pipelines still require population coverage and binding
evidence. The favorable measured tradeoff does not establish
history-independent cost: new properties can require acquisition,
and population work can grow with scope even under an execution cap.

\finding{The measured tradeoff shifts in favor of structured memory as
history grows: call counts stay fixed while latency diverges. Coverage
and acquisition obligations still limit any claim of constant full cost.}
